# Splits — frozen, do not regenerate

Generated by `../scripts/make_splits.py` (seed 42), audited by `../scripts/audit_splits.py`.
Full statistics in `split_report.json`.

**Every experiment reports on `peptide_split`.** If you think the split is wrong, open an experiment
arguing that — do not quietly make your own. Numbers from different splits cannot be compared, and
the brief judges us on exactly this kind of discipline.

## How to use

```python
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("context/dataset.csv")
sp = pd.read_csv("splits/peptide_split.csv")        # peptide, cluster_id, split
df = df.merge(sp, on="peptide")                      # adds a `split` column

train = df[df.split == "train"]                      # 22,532 rows
val   = df[df.split == "val"]                        #  2,817 rows
test  = df[df.split == "test"]                       #  2,817 rows
```

Use `val` for model selection and early stopping. **Touch `test` once, at the end.** Picking a model
on the test set reintroduces, by hand, the leakage this whole exercise exists to prevent.

## The leakage this prevents

Rows are (peptide, allele) pairs. A peptide is measured against a median of 4 alleles and up to 36,
and **94% of rows involve a peptide that appears with more than one allele**. Split rows at random
and the same fragment lands in train and test, so the model recognises it rather than predicting it.

So we split by peptide, and group near-duplicate peptides so they travel together.

## Why the threshold is Hamming ≤ 2, not the conventional ≤ 1

The published convention for this dataset is 80% sequence identity. Every peptide is a 9-mer, and
for equal-length strings a Levenshtein distance of 1 is reachable only by substitution, so
`identity = (9 − d)/9` gives 0.889 at d=1 and 0.778 at d=2. **80% identity on 9-mers is exactly
Hamming ≤ 1.**

We adopted a convention and then checked whether it holds. It does not. Measuring how well a test
peptide's label is predicted by its nearest training peptide — as a within-allele z-score of
`log1p`, so allele-level differences cannot inflate it (`measure_leakage_by_distance.py`):

| edit distance | identity | Spearman with neighbour |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.778 | **0.700** |
| 3 | 0.667 | 0.311 |
| 4 | 0.556 | 0.364 |
| random pair | — | 0.027 |

The break between "near-duplicate" and "merely similar" sits **between 2 and 3**, not between 1 and
2. A threshold of 1 would have left 13 test peptides whose labels were ~70% rank-predictable from a
training neighbour. Cost of the stricter choice is negligible — 5,410 clusters instead of 5,494, and
the largest cluster grows from 5 peptides to 8, so there is no single-linkage chaining problem.

Re-measured on the final split, the near-duplicate regime is gone and what remains is a flat band
consistent with genuine biological similarity:

| edit distance | Spearman | note |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 0.322 | closest any eval peptide now gets to train |
| 4 | 0.304 | |
| 5 | 0.239 | |
| random pair | −0.016 | the null, which validates the measurement |

**That null matters.** A first version of this measurement pooled pairs across alleles and reported
a null of 0.335 — which should have been ~0. Alleles differ systematically in stability, so both
members of every pair carried their allele's mean and correlated for that reason alone. The entire
table was confounded. Normalising within allele fixed it. A probe whose null is not ~0 is not
measuring what it claims.

## Two splits, two different questions

| File | Holds out | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| `peptide_split.csv` | **PRIMARY** — whole peptide clusters | Does the model generalise to *new fragments*? |
| `allele_split.csv` | whole alleles | Does it generalise to an *HLA it never saw*? |

The allele split is a secondary analysis, not a replacement. It is the more interesting test of the
brief's actual question — a protein foundation model should beat a one-hot allele encoding precisely
where the allele is unseen, because one-hot cannot represent it at all. Never mix numbers between
the two splits.

## Audit results

`python scripts/audit_splits.py` re-derives everything from the CSVs by brute force, assuming
nothing from the generator (which verifies itself with the same index it built the clusters from —
a shared bug would hide from both).

- **Brute-force O(n²) neighbour search**, every eval peptide against every training peptide: the
  closest is **3 edits away**; zero sit at ≤2.
- **val ↔ test** are also not near-duplicates of each other (closest 3 edits).
- No peptide in two splits; no cluster spans sides; all 5,633 peptides assigned exactly once.
- **The leak, quantified.** A predictor that does nothing but look up the same peptide's mean label
  in training — no features, no ability to generalise — finds its peptide for **93.6% of eval rows
  on a random row split and scores Spearman 0.337** there. On our split it finds **0.0%** and scores
  **0.000**. That gap is the leak a random split would have handed us.
- **The detector is validated before it is trusted.** Each generator run plants a known-positive —
  a training peptide with one residue mutated, labelled as test — and the check must flag it. A
  probe that cannot fail is not measuring anything.

## Representativeness

| split | rows | % | median t½ | zero fraction | alleles | alleles/peptide | KS vs train |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| train | 22,532 | 80.00 | 1.10 | 0.201 | 75 | 4.99 | — |
| val | 2,817 | 10.00 | 1.00 | 0.206 | 73 | 5.00 | 0.013 |
| test | 2,817 | 10.00 | 1.10 | 0.201 | 75 | 5.07 | 0.008 |

That `alleles/peptide` column was a bug worth recording. The first version ranked splits by
**absolute** quota deficit; since train's quota is 8× the others', it took every large cluster, and
test ended up with no peptide measured against more than 6 alleles while train reached 36 —
structurally unrepresentative even though the row counts were a perfect 80/10/10. Ranking by
**fraction of quota remaining** fixed it. The generator now asserts the ratio stays within
0.85–1.15; the buggy version scores 0.36 and the fixed one 1.00, so the guard separates the two
cases rather than merely existing.

## Caveat on the allele split

| split | rows | % | median t½ | alleles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| train | 22,039 | 78.25 | 1.10 | 64 |
| val | 3,068 | 10.89 | 0.70 | 5 |
| test | 3,059 | 10.86 | 1.30 | 6 |

Alleles are assigned within terciles of their median half-life, which keeps the label distributions
roughly aligned — without stratification, test landed at median 2.90 against train's 0.90. Some
shift remains and is unavoidable: there are only 75 alleles, so an eval split holds 5 or 6, and
alleles genuinely differ in their stability profiles. **Treat allele-split numbers as a directional
signal, not as comparable to peptide-split numbers.**

## What this split does *not* control for

Stated so nobody discovers it in Q&A:

- **Shared anchor motifs.** Peptides binding the same allele share residues at the anchor positions.
  Two peptides can be 5 edits apart and still share both anchors. We do not cluster on anchors, and
  neither does any published work on this dataset — that similarity is the signal a model is
  supposed to learn, not leakage. But it does mean "unseen peptide" is not "unrelated peptide".
- **The HLA side is shared by construction.** All 75 alleles appear in training, intentionally:
  `peptide_split` measures generalisation to new fragments, not to new alleles. Use `allele_split`
  for the latter.
- **Assay.** Every row is one assay (SPA at 37 °C) from one source, so nothing here measures
  cross-laboratory generalisation. Published work finds that is where accuracy collapses.

## Regenerating

```
python scripts/make_splits.py        # deterministic given SEED = 42
python scripts/audit_splits.py       # independent check; exits non-zero on failure
```

The generator asserts its own invariants and will fail rather than emit a leaky split. If you change
`CLUSTER_MAX_DIST` or `TARGETS`, you have made a different split — say so loudly, because every
experiment already run becomes incomparable.
